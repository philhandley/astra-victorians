How the homework works, how to print the sheets, and where every fact and picture came from.

How the points work

The homework sheet asks for four tasks a term, and more are welcome. Points are 5, 10 or 15 per task, plus a bonus for the landmark visit, and extra points for effort, creativity and presentation. Totals are added up at the end of term for a class prize.

Everything goes in the green homework book. Models and anything 3D need photos, because points are collected from the book. Your own homework ideas are welcome too: add them to the task list so the teacher knows.

The tick boxes on this site are just for us. They are saved on this device only and are never sent anywhere.

Printing the sheets

  • Every sheet is laid out for A4. Open it and press Print, or download the PDF.
  • In the print dialog choose Actual size or 100%, not "fit to page", so the handwriting lines stay 12 mm and the model net stays the right size.
  • Margins are built in. Set the printer margins to none or default; either works.
  • The bathing-machine net and the booklet are better on card or thicker paper.
  • The booklet and the timeline are landscape; the PDFs are already turned the right way.
  • No printer? Most sheets can be copied by hand onto plain paper: the frames are just boxes.

How the facts were checked

Every fact on a task page or printable has a source. The research notes behind the site are in the research/ folder of the project: one brief per topic, each line with its link, and anything that could not be confirmed marked as unverified and left off the sheets. Dates for Queen Victoria follow the Royal Collection Trust, English Heritage and royal.uk; school history follows BBC Bitesize, the National Archives and museum education packs; local facts follow Historic England list entries, the Canal & River Trust and the venues' own websites.

Some things are deliberately gentle. Victorian punishments are named but not described. Child labour is mentioned as "some children had jobs instead of school", which is enough for six.

The "look inside" models

The two schools and the bathing machine are 3D models you can spin round with a finger. Tap any part and a card says what it is, with a picture, a Read aloud button and its source. Big buttons ring the bell, light the fire, lift the roof off, or cut the building open: an axo cutaway that slices every wall at about child height and shows the cut in white, like an architect's plan. On the bathing machine you wind the winch to roll it down its grooved stone rails into the sea, open the curtains and send the bather for a swim. The people move: they walk, wave, run round the playground, swim breaststroke and tread water. There are people in every model and a few "can you find…?" questions, because that is what the children's cutaway books (Biesty, Usborne's See Inside) do.

Beside each model is the real thing: Clarke's own 1852 drawing and plan of each school, and photographs of the bathing machine. "Turn the model to match" swings the model round to the same view, so you can compare them.

The schools are real buildings from Joseph Clarke's pattern book Schools and School Houses (1852, public domain, scanned by the Getty Research Institute): Monks Horton in Kent, a single room for 50 children, and Lydd in Kent, two rooms for 200 children with the master's house attached. Room sizes are read from Clarke's plans; heights are measured from his sections and rounded. The bathing machine's length and width are the real 12 feet by 7 feet (Emsworth Museum); its other sizes, colours and iron brackets are measured by eye from photographs of the restored machine at Osborne. It ran on grooved stone rails down a slipway and was let down and hauled back by a winch and wire rope (Historic England Archive, English Heritage); the slipway's height and the rails' size are our guesses.

The models are built by Python scripts in Blender (tools/blender/) and exported as glTF (assets/models/), then shown with three.js from a CDN. They need a reasonably recent tablet or computer.
